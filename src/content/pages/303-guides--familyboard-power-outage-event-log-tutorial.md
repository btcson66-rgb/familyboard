# How to Use FamilyBoard for a Power Outage Record

“The power was out for a while” is a memory, not a useful handoff. During a real interruption, the household may notice the lights at one time, see a utility notice later, keep a refrigerator closed, regain internet after the lights and find one device behaving differently the next morning. FamilyBoard can keep those observations in order without pretending that a household timestamp is an official outage record.

This tutorial pairs with the [Household Power Outage Event Log](/tools/household-power-outage-event-log/). It shows how to use the app for a bounded local record: one event code, one review date, source pointers, system observations and a named next check. It does not monitor the grid, contact a utility, inspect wiring, calculate a safety result or replace a qualified plan.

## 1. Start with a private event code

Open the tool and enter a neutral label such as `OUTAGE-HOME-A` or `POWER-EVENT-1`. Do not use a street address, account number, meter number, phone number or login in the label. Choose the scope that matches what you can support: an official area event, a shared building system, one dwelling or an unresolved scope. Scope describes an observation; it is not a diagnosis.

Keep one interruption separate from another. A short event on Monday, a building generator test and a second interruption on Wednesday need different records because their sources, times and owners may differ. Combining them creates a clean-looking timeline that cannot answer which device or notice belonged to which event.

## 2. Record the first supportable observation

Enter the date and 24-hour time when someone actually noticed the condition. If a blinking clock suggests the interruption began earlier, record that as an observation or estimate in the evidence note rather than silently changing the start to a precise time. The form checks that the date and time are real and not in the future; it does not infer an official start.

Name the source and the channel you checked, for example a utility outage page, a building notice or a protected household note. A neighborhood message can help you find the right page, but it is not automatically the responsible source. Keep the full account or notice in a protected location and put only a stable pointer in the FamilyBoard row.

## 3. Add system rows from what was actually seen

Use one row for one operational unit: dwelling supply, lighting, refrigerator/freezer, home network, accessibility support or another real dependency. Write observable language such as “router indicators were off while the dwelling supply was unavailable.” Do not turn that into “the router failed” unless the responsible source established it.

Each row has an ID, an observed condition, an evidence pointer, an action already taken, an owner and a status. A dated photo index, device indicator or protected temperature record may support a row; the tool does not require you to enter the original file or private content. If a condition cannot be checked safely, record that it was not accessible and leave the row open.

## 4. Treat restoration as a handoff, not a finish line

When electricity appears again, change the event stage to restoration observed and enter the actual restoration-observed time. That moment does not prove every circuit, appliance, network or care dependency is ready. Use `Restored; recheck pending` for a system that still needs a normal check, and `Official or qualified follow-up pending` when the answer belongs to a utility, health authority, pharmacist, clinician, manufacturer or qualified electrical professional.

Do not use this app to decide whether refrigerated contents, medication or a power-dependent device may be used. Preserve the time, relevant source pointer and household observation, then follow the current responsible guidance. If there is an exposed line, water near electricity, burning smell, structural danger or another immediate hazard, stop collecting evidence and use the current local emergency or qualified-professional route.

## 5. Give every open ID one next action

The follow-up row connects an unresolved ID to one evidence-based action, one household role and a due date within the review window. “Check again” is not enough. “Recheck the serving utility status at the next planned interval and save the new source timestamp” can be verified. “Decide whether food is safe” is too broad; point instead to the current health authority or product-specific source that must make that decision.

Close a row only after the stated recheck happened and its supporting pointer is preserved. If the next review reveals a different condition, keep the original observation and create a new dated row or event version. Editing history into a successful outcome removes the very context a future household member may need.

## 6. Review the export before sharing

Before copying, downloading or printing, remove addresses, account and meter identifiers, phone numbers, access codes, medical details, device credentials and private messages. A local-first result is still a new copy once you save it elsewhere. Review who may see it, where the protected originals live and whether an old printed copy should be replaced.

The [emergency supply audit](/tools/emergency-supply-inventory-audit/) checks physical supplies against a current plan. This outage-log tutorial answers the narrower App question: how to keep event facts, sources and follow-up separate while the household works through restoration.

## Affiliate boundary

Future recommendations for labels, storage or general backup accessories may appear after the educational content with a clear affiliate disclosure and an easy skip. They cannot appear inside the input or result, choose a generator or battery, certify a home, or imply that purchasing something changes an outage status. The entire workflow remains usable without a purchase.
