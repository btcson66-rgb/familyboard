# A home has a history, but most of it disappears into receipts and memory

A new owner, renter, spouse or technician eventually asks the same questions: When was this installed? Has it been repaired before? Who worked on it? What part was replaced? Where is the receipt? Was the problem recurring? A home record keeper gives those questions a durable place to start without pretending that a household needs to digitize every file it owns.

A useful record is a timeline plus a source map. It says what happened, which asset or room it affected, who observed or owned the next action, and where the formal evidence lives. FamilyBoard stores that working context locally in the current browser; it does not become a cloud archive of contracts, photos or receipts.

## Record events around the home

Useful events include purchases, installations, maintenance, repairs, inspections, renovations and replacements. Each event can connect to an asset, room, contractor or document reference. Use a stable label such as `HVAC-01` or `KITCHEN-REPAIR-2026-A`, then add the event date, the date confidence, the responsible role and a short description of what was actually observed.

Keep facts separate from interpretation. “Filter replaced on 2026-04-12; invoice pointer INV-A4” is an observed record. “This should prevent every summer failure” is a prediction and does not belong in the same field. When a provider, manual or invoice controls a detail, keep a pointer to that source rather than rewriting its wording from memory.

## History reduces repeated detective work

When an appliance fails twice, prior notes may show what changed between visits. When a contractor returns, the previous work order gives context without asking the household to retell the entire story. When you prepare to move, a renovation and service history can be summarized by asset and date instead of reconstructed from a box of mixed receipts.

The timeline is also useful for handoff. A substitute household manager should be able to find the next source and understand what remains open, while sensitive details such as payment credentials, full addresses and private correspondence stay in protected storage. A record keeper is not a reason to expose the whole household to everyone who needs one small fact.

## Keep facts separate from guesses

If you do not know when something was installed, mark it unknown or approximate rather than inventing a date. Note why the estimate exists — a dated photo, a prior owner's note, a service label — and what would confirm it. A reliable partial record is more useful than a complete-looking record filled with assumptions, especially when a warranty, insurance question or future sale makes the distinction important.

Do not silently merge duplicate assets. If two records might be the same water heater, keep both until the model, location or source is checked. Close the duplicate with a reason and a link to the surviving record. That small audit trail prevents later users from mistaking an administrative cleanup for a physical replacement.

## Start from now

You do not need to reconstruct ten years of household history before the system becomes useful. Start with the most recent repair or major purchase, add the source pointer and next review, then test whether another household member can find it without coaching. Old receipts can be added later when they are easy to identify. If an event cannot be connected to a real source, leave it out or mark the gap explicitly.

The current browser is the storage boundary. Export and test a backup before clearing site data or moving to another browser; there is no account recovery service that can restore a local timeline. Future affiliate recommendations for folders, labels or scanning equipment must remain outside the record itself, clearly disclosed and optional. Buying storage supplies cannot make an unverified date true or replace an independent backup.
