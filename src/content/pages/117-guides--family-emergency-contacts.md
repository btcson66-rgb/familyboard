# An emergency contact list should answer “who do I call next?”

Long address books are not emergency plans. A focused contact list is a quick routing card: it helps a specific person decide who to call next when the usual household manager is unavailable. That makes it different from a full emergency information sheet, which may also cover meeting places, medications, evacuation details and document sources. Keep this page narrow enough to read in under a minute, then link to the deeper plan when the situation requires it.

Before entering a number, decide who will read the list, where it will live and what action each role supports. A refrigerator copy for household members can include more context than a school pickup card or a sitter's sheet. Do not place every personal contact in every version; a small audience-specific list is easier to update and less likely to expose someone who never agreed to be included.

## Use clear contact roles

Label contacts as household member, nearby trusted person, building manager, utility outage, veterinarian, caregiver or another role that explains why the number matters. A role should tell the reader what to ask for: “building manager — water shutoff and access” is more useful than a name with no context. If two people share a role, mark the primary and backup order instead of leaving the reader to guess.

For each entry, keep only the fields needed for the next action: display name or organization, role, preferred channel, backup channel when it is genuinely different, and the date the source was checked. A service number copied from an old invoice is not the same as a current official outage line. Keep the controlling source pointer in the private household record so a reviewer can verify the number without trusting the printed copy forever.

## Include more than one communication path where appropriate

A mobile number, office line or official service website can provide alternatives, but do not create false redundancy by copying the same channel twice. Record what to do if there is no answer: leave a message, call the official dispatch line, contact the nearby backup or follow the local emergency service instruction. In Taiwan, verify 110, 119 and 112 against current official guidance; in another country, replace them with the local authority's instructions. A FamilyBoard page cannot determine which service is appropriate for a live incident.

Use neutral notes rather than private explanations. “Neighbour has the spare key; ask first” is an operational instruction. A diagnosis, family conflict, access code or account credential does not belong on a broadly visible card. Mark contacts that should not appear in a handoff as sensitive in the household record; do not try to bypass that boundary by pasting the number into a note.

## Make an offline copy

A printable sheet or exported offline view can be useful if internet access is limited. Keep one copy where the intended reader can reach it and a protected source copy where the household maintains the authoritative details. Put a prepared date and review-by trigger on the card. If a move, school change, care arrangement, phone-number change or loss of consent occurs, retire the old copy immediately rather than waiting for the annual review.

Treat PDF and print destinations as new holders of household information. Check the printer queue, shared folder and message recipient before sending. FamilyBoard stores records in the current browser and does not create a cloud sharing permission for this list; any PDF, email or messaging-app copy follows that service's access and retention rules.

## Test the list

Ask another household member to locate a specific contact without coaching: “Who do you call about a water leak after hours?” Watch whether they can identify the role, choose the first channel and name the backup step. If they search by a person's name or ask what the label means, rewrite the role or group the entries differently. A test result is evidence about the list; it is not evidence that the provider will answer during a real outage.

After the drill, record the date, the entry tested, what the reader found and what changed. This tiny review log prevents a polished card from becoming a forgotten artifact. Keep the list deliberately small, and use the Emergency Information Sheet when the reader also needs a meeting plan, evacuation details or protected-document pointers.
