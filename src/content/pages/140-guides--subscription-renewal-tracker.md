# A renewal reminder should create a decision window

A notification after the card is charged is too late to be useful. A renewal tracker should create a decision window: enough time to check the current price, identify the account owner, read the cancellation route and decide whether the household still wants the service. It is not a banking connection and it is not proof that a seller sent a notice.

Start with the services that create the most surprise—annual software, streaming bundles, storage, memberships and subscription boxes—rather than trying to catalogue every small charge in one sitting. For each item, record the service name, billing frequency, next renewal date, notice or cancellation lead time, responsible person, management source and the decision that is still open. Keep payment credentials and full card numbers out of the household record.

## Annual renewals need longer visibility

A yearly service can disappear from household awareness for eleven months. Surface it well before renewal—often four to six weeks ahead—so the owner can compare the current plan, ask other household members whether it is still used and complete any cancellation or downgrade process before the charge. If the seller's notice window is shorter, the household's own review date should still be earlier; a missed email is not a reliable workflow.

Record the source version and date you checked. A price seen in an old welcome email is not the current renewal price. If the service is billed through an app store, record that management route rather than assuming the service's website can cancel it.

## Monthly renewals may not need individual alerts

For stable monthly subscriptions, a quarterly or monthly review may be less noisy than constant notifications. The right cadence depends on price volatility, cancellation friction and how often the household evaluates the service. If a price change or trial conversion would cause real harm, use a more specific lead-time task; if the amount is stable and low, a bounded review can preserve attention without creating notification fatigue.

## Track changes

If a price changes, the plan is downgraded, the owner changes or the billing route moves to a different store, update the active record and preserve the dated change. Do not create duplicates every time a seller changes a tier. The history should show what the household believed at each review, while the active row answers what needs a decision now.

Separate preparation from outcome. “Open the provider's cancel page before 2026-10-01” is a household action. “Cancellation accepted and access ends 2026-10-03” is an observed external result that needs confirmation. “Refund requested” is not “refund received”; keep those states distinct.

## Keep cancellation notes factual

Management links, account owner, renewal date and a plain-language cancellation route are useful. Passwords, payment numbers, security answers and private messages are not. Use a neutral source ID and store the protected original in the appropriate password manager or account system. FamilyBoard does not connect to financial accounts or cancel a subscription for you.

After a renewal decision, record the result and the next review trigger. If the household keeps the service, note why and when to look again. If it is cancelled, record the confirmation and the date to verify that the next statement no longer contains the charge. A tracker that only says “cancelled” can still miss a billing-system error.
